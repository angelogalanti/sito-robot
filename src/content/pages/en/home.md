A humanoid in your living room or shop impresses everyone, but the environment decides whether it stays on or ends up in a corner. In 2026 no humanoid performs open-ended chores without supervision: check spaces, battery and who follows it before buying for a place, not for a video.

> **In brief:** at home, stairs, free space and hours of presence matter; at work, supervision, liability and support matter. The battery lasts one or two hours: no model works a full shift alone.[1](#sources)

## What they can really do in 2026

Walking, greeting, dancing on command, carrying small objects on known routes. Open-ended chores — clearing tables, tidying different rooms, caregiving — remain out of reach without supervision: the most up-to-date independent roundup says so clearly.[1](#sources) A demonstration video does not prove the robot repeats the task at your home or in your shop.

## Home: stairs, space and battery

A home humanoid must live with stairs, rugs, thresholds, children and pets. The manufacturer asks users to keep a safe distance between the robot and people, and forbids dangerous modifications.[2](#sources) Measure your spaces first: an R1 is 123 cm tall, a K1 95 cm at 19.5 kg.[1](#sources)[3](#sources)

The battery decides how long it stays on: about two hours for the G1, about one hour for the R1, up to 70 minutes of walking for the K1.[1](#sources)[3](#sources) Plan where it charges and who puts it back on charge: without a person following it, real runtime is that of an appliance, not a coworker.

## Business: reception, events and shops

In business, humanoids work as an attraction: reception, trade fairs, events, photos with customers. These are supervised roles, with an operator nearby and closed tasks (greeting, pointing, entertaining). They do not replace staff and do not work alone in public spaces: the environment needs a risk assessment, like any equipment.

If the robot records video or voices of customers, normal privacy rules apply: inform visitors and limit recordings to what is needed.

## Safety: what to ask first

For personal care robots there is a dedicated standard, ISO 13482, covering domestic robots, physical assistants and person carriers, harmonised with the Machinery Directive.[4](#sources) It is not a sticker on the product: ask the seller which safety assessments exist for the model and configuration you buy.

With children and pets, the manufacturer's rule applies: safe distance and no dangerous uses.[2](#sources) The base G1 is not programmable, and small stature with about 2 kg of payload rules out heavy work: these are limits to know beforehand, not flaws to discover later.[1](#sources)

## Before buying for a space: five written answers

1. Exact model dimensions and weight, and whether it fits your doors, corridors and lifts.
2. Real runtime in your use case, and charging times.
3. Who supervises it, who recharges it and who steps in if it falls or gets stuck.
4. What is included: hands, sensors, software, training and support in Europe.
5. A live test in the real environment or a clear written return policy, as with any distance purchase.[5](#sources)
